The question in each case: which lever moves unit economics most?

## Case 1. Ads got twice as expensive

```case
? A metal-nesting web service; customer LTV 12,000; nearly all customers come from paid search.
? CAC rose from 3000 to 6000 in six months as competitors entered the auction; LTV/CAC fell from 4 to 2.
+ Build channels the auction doesn't price: referrals, content and SEO, partnerships with metal suppliers; raise LTV through retention :: One paid channel lets competitors set your margin. +50% LTV restores a ratio of 3.
~ Raise the price 20% :: Helps if churn holds; test it, but the channel problem remains.
- Double the ad budget :: The auction gets pricier and each customer barely pays back.
```

## Case 2. Customers keep leaving

```case
? Maintenance-tracking SaaS: 5000 a month, 80% margin, CAC 20,000.
? Churn is 8% a month; the board wants more sales.
+ Fix churn first: call churned customers, find where they drop off, improve onboarding :: LTV = 5000 × 0.8 / 0.08 = 50,000, ratio 2.5; halving churn doubles LTV with no extra ad spend.
~ Annual plan at 15% off :: Cash upfront, but unhappy customers leave together a year later.
- Hire two more salespeople :: The base caps at new customers / churn; you only replace leavers.
```

## Case 3. A year of building, no customers

```case
? Three engineers designed a vibration sensor for predictive maintenance and want 12 months to build the full product before meeting customers.
? Runway is 15 months.
+ Spend 1–2 months on 20–30 interviews and sell 2–3 paid pilots with manual analysis (concierge MVP) :: The main risk is whether and for what customers will pay.
~ Build a simple MVP in 3–4 months, then sell :: Better, still builds before validating.
- Follow the plan; a good product sells itself :: The most common way startups die.
```
