## The whole topic in a minute

- "Draw the missing view" is **detective work**: from two views you rebuild the part in your head and draw the third.
- Method 1: **number the corners** and carry them across along projection lines.
- Method 2: **start with a solid block** and mentally saw off the bits that aren't there.
- A cylinder cut by a plane: along the axis you get a rectangle, across it a circle, **at a slant an ellipse**. At 45° the ellipse turns into a circle in the neighbouring view.
- These are test 1 exercises 1 and 2 (1.5 and 2.5 points) and part of 3. An outline mistake gives **0 points** for the whole exercise.

## How the views are linked

Three views are three shadows of one part, so:

- **height** is the same in the front and side views (horizontal projection lines);
- **width** is the same in the front and top views (vertical projection lines);
- **depth** is the same in the top and side views. Carry it over with a compass or a 45° mitre line.

Analogy: placing furniture from a floor plan. The plan (top view) tells you where the wardrobe is in width and depth; the side view tells you its height.

## Method 1: number the points

1. Find the same corner in both given views and label it with the same number.
2. Draw projection lines from it to where the third view goes. Where they cross is its third projection.
3. Repeat for every corner, then join the points that are joined on the part.
4. Check every line: visible (wide) or hidden behind something (dashed)?

Slow but reliable. In the test it saves you when your imagination gives up.

## Method 2: saw it out of a block

This is what the lecturer recommends in lecture 8 before the test:

1. Draw the **bounding block** the part fits in (in the test usually a rectangular or square prism).
2. Look at the views for places **where there is no material**: a cut-out, a step, a slot.
3. Mentally saw those pieces off. Every cut in one view must show up somewhere in the others.
4. Do a quick isometric sketch on scrap paper: one minute that saves ten.

Analogy: a sculptor takes a block of marble and removes everything that isn't the statue.

## Prism, pyramid, cylinder

- **Prism**: all edges along one axis are parallel. The view along the axis shows the cross-section (a polygon); the others show rectangles.
- **Pyramid**: edges meet at the apex. The top view shows the base with edges running to the centre; the others show triangles.
- **Cylinder**: a circle along the axis, a rectangle from the side. **The centre line is compulsory** in every view.

## Cutting a cylinder with a plane

![A cylinder cut by an oblique plane](/drawing/cylinder-cut.svg "Oblique cut: a circle in the top view, an ellipse in the side view")

This is test exercise 2. Three cases:

| Plane | Shape of the cut |
|---|---|
| Parallel to the axis | Rectangle (a flat, a slot). In the view along the axis it's a straight chord |
| Perpendicular to the axis | Circle (just a shorter cylinder, an end face) |
| Oblique | **Ellipse** |

Building the ellipse:

1. In the view where the plane is seen edge-on (a slanted line), mark the lowest point (1), the highest (3) and the middle ones (2 and 4, on the axis).
2. In the view along the axis these points lie on the circle, because the cut is still on the cylinder's surface.
3. Carry each point's height across to the third view. One half-axis of the ellipse equals the cylinder's **radius**, the other equals **half the height** of the cut.
4. Add points in between (8–12 around the circle) and join them smoothly, freehand or with a French curve.

The slope sets the shape: under 45° the ellipse lies flat, exactly 45° gives a **circle**, over 45° the ellipse stands up. That's the α < 45°, α = 45°, α > 45° slide from seminar 3.

Analogy: slice a sausage at an angle and the piece is oval. The steeper the cut, the longer the oval.

## True size and developments

- A slanted face looks **squashed** in the normal views. To see its real shape, look square onto it (an auxiliary view). Take lengths along the edge from the view where the face is seen edge-on, and the width from the neighbouring view.
- A **development (mantle)** is the surface's "sewing pattern": cut the prism along an edge and unroll it on the table. For a prism it's a strip of rectangles as long as the base perimeter; for a cylinder it's a rectangle πD wide.

## Tangents (from HW 1)

Seminar 2 covered joining lines and arcs properly. "Lines not tangent" and "endpoints don't match" are the most common mistakes.

**Tangent from point A to a circle with centre O:**
1. Join A to O.
2. Find the midpoint B of AO.
3. With centre B and radius BA, draw an arc. It cuts the circle at C.
4. AC is the tangent. Angle OCA is a right angle because it stands on a diameter.

Rule for the final drawing: **arcs first, then straight lines**, and find the tangent point by construction, not by eye.
