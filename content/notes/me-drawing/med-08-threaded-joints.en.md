## The whole topic in a minute

- **First rule:** where a bolt is screwed into a nut or a hole, **the bolt's thread covers the hole's thread**. You see the bolt's lines; the hole's lines hide beneath them.
- A hole the bolt merely passes through is **bigger than the bolt** (clearance hole). The gap is visible.
- Bolts, nuts and washers are **not hatched** in section; they stay as a view.
- A **stud** is screwed into a blind hole to the end of its thread, with a nut on top.
- **Thread locking** against self-loosening: spring washer, jam nut, castle nut with cotter pin, tab washer.
- This is test 1 exercise 4, 3 points.

## What exists

**Bolts and screws:** hexagon head (the most common), square head, fitted bolt, socket head (Allen key), countersunk, round head, cheese head, set screw (no head), eye bolt, hammer head. Ends can be flat, round, tapered or pinned.

**Nuts:** hexagon, dome, castle (for a cotter pin), flange, self-locking with a nylon ring, square, wing, slotted round bearing locknut, eye nut, knurled.

## Drawing a hexagon

The corners of a hex head are cut by a **120° cone**. This is **not a chamfer**: the hexagonal prism meeting a cone gives hyperbolas on the faces. On the drawing they are replaced by **circular arcs**. In the top view the cone shows as a circle **inscribed** in the hexagon.

Simplified sketch proportions (not exact standard values): across-flats about **1.6–2 d**, nut height about **0.8 d**, head height about **0.7 d**.

Tip: build it in CAD (extrude a hexagon, cut with a revolve) and you'll understand the arcs forever.

## Joints

![Bolt, washer and nut through two plates, in section](/drawing/bolted-joint.svg "The classic: a bolt through two clearance holes")

**1. Bolt and nut through clearance holes.** Both parts have a plain hole with a gap. The bolt passes through, washer and nut below. The bolt sticks out of the nut by 1–2 pitches. The plates are hatched in opposite directions.

**2. Bolt into a blind tapped hole.** Top part with a clearance hole, bottom part tapped. The thread is deeper than the bolt goes in, the drilled hole deeper still, with a 120° cone at the bottom. Where the bolt is engaged you see **the bolt's lines** (first rule).

**3. Stud bolt.** Used when a through bolt won't fit and you don't want to wear out the thread in the housing (often cast iron or aluminium) at every disassembly. The stud is **screwed in to the end of its thread** and stays in the housing; only the nut comes off. The screwed-in length depends on the housing material: about 1d in steel, 1.25d in cast iron, 2d in aluminium.

**4. Socket head screw.** The head sits in a **counterbore** (a cylindrical recess).

**5. Countersunk screw.** The head sits in a **countersink** (a conical recess), flush with the top.

The parts list gives diameter and length, **M8×35**, plus the standard number.

## Thread locking

Vibration can undo a nut. So:

- **Spring (lock) washer:** a split ring that springs and bites with its ends. Toothed washers bite the surface with teeth.
- **Jam nut:** two nuts on top of each other. The lower one is jammed against the upper one: the bolt touches opposite thread flanks in the two nuts and can't move.
- **Castle nut + cotter pin:** slots in the nut, a hole in the bolt. The pin goes through and its ends are **bent over**. The nut physically can't turn. Wheels and critical parts are held like this.
- **Tab washer:** the washer's edge is **bent up** against a flat of the nut or head. The bent tab is a rib, so it is **not cut** in section.
- **Bearing locknut + lockwasher:** the washer's inner tooth sits in a groove on the shaft, and one outer tab is bent into a slot of the nut.
- **Lock wire** through the heads of several bolts (aviation).

For a sense of scale: the "Jesus nut" on a helicopter is one nut holding the whole rotor. If it comes off, all that's left is to pray. That's why locking is no detail.

## Union fitting

Joins two pipes: one end has a thread, the other a **collar**. The union nut slides onto the collared pipe and screws onto the thread, pulling the collars together. The pipes themselves don't turn. In seminar 7 it was drawn half view – half section.

What gets checked:
- the **run-off groove** at the end of the thread;
- the **collar diameters**: the nut must slide over the pipe but catch the collar;
- diameter differences between parts, so you can see where one ends and the next begins.

## What the test asks (exercise 4)

You get two parts and "join them": e.g. a hex bolt with spring washer and nut through two plates, or a stud with a nut in a blind hole. Castle nut with cotter pin, bearing nut with lockwasher or a union fitting can also come up. What's checked:

1. The bolt hole is **bigger** than the bolt, the gap is visible.
2. **First rule**: the bolt's lines show in the thread.
3. The tapped hole is **deeper** than the bolt goes in, the drilling deeper still, 120° cone.
4. Fasteners are **not hatched**.
5. Neighbouring parts hatched in **opposite directions**.
6. Wide and narrow lines in the right places. This now counts more than before.
