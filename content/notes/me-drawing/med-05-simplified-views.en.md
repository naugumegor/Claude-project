## The whole topic in a minute

- A drawing is "economic": draw **only what adds new information**.
- A turned part often needs **one view** with a centre line and the Ø sign.
- A symmetric part can be drawn as a **half or a quarter**, marking the symmetry with two short strokes on the axis.
- A tiny area gets a **detail view** at its own scale.
- Repeated holes and teeth are drawn once or twice; the rest are shown by centre lines.
- Castings have no sharp edges, but **imaginary intersection lines** are drawn as narrow lines that stop short of the outline.

## Turned parts

A shaft doesn't need a left view, it's just circles. The centre line and the **Ø** in front of the dimension say "this is round". A diameter is always measured across the line of sight.

## Symmetric parts

- A part symmetric about one axis can be drawn as a **half**; symmetric about two axes, as a **quarter**.
- At the ends of the symmetry line put **two short narrow strokes** across it: the sign that the other half was left out.
- If an outline falls on the symmetry line, break it with a **zig-zag**.
- If the halves differ in a small detail, say so in a note (better avoided).

Analogy: you don't have to draw the whole butterfly, one wing and a note "the other is the same" is enough.

## Partial and local views

- **Part view:** draw only the important piece of a view, broken off with a freehand line.
- **Local view:** a small view of one feature, e.g. a slot. By the BME rule it is projected **in third angle**, placed next to the feature and tied to the main view by a centre line. Not used in CAD.
- **Detail view:** a circle around a small area, a letter, and the area drawn larger nearby, e.g. **B (5:1)**.

## Repeated features

A row of identical holes, ribs or teeth is drawn fully **once or twice** (often at the ends); the others are located by **centre lines** or a thin outline. The count goes into the dimension: "6× Ø8".

## Rotated and angled views

- A slanted face is distorted in the normal views. Draw an **auxiliary view** square onto it, marked with an arrow and a letter.
- A view may be **rotated** to sit better on the sheet. Then the rotation symbol and angle go next to the letter.

## Knurling, flats, bending

- **Knurling** on handles: show a patch of the narrow-line grid and label it.
- **Flat surface** on a round part: mark the flat with **two narrow crossing diagonals**. Otherwise nobody can tell it's a plane, not a cylinder.
- **Flattened view** of a bent part: show the blank before bending, bend lines narrow double-dotted. There's a dedicated "flattened" symbol.

## Double-dotted lines

The narrow long-dash double-dotted line draws things that "aren't there" in the usual sense:

- the **initial outline** before bending or machining;
- **adjacent parts** needed for understanding (as transparent silhouettes);
- the **end position** of a moving part (a lever "up" and "down");
- a spring or flexible element in its working state.

## Imaginary intersection lines

Cast and forged parts have no sharp edges, everything is rounded. But if you draw nothing, nobody can tell where one surface ends and the next begins.

So you draw an **imaginary intersection line** where the surfaces would meet **without the fillet**. It is a **narrow** line and **stops a couple of millimetres short** of the outline.

- 3D CAD draws **tangent edges** instead, where the curvature changes. Hand-drawn 2D drawings don't show them.
- If the slope is shallow and two imaginary lines would almost merge, draw **one wide** line.
- Coaxial round surfaces don't need imaginary lines.

Analogy: the seam on a soft sofa. No sharp corner, but you can see where the back ends and the seat begins.
