## The whole topic in a minute

- A **caliper** measures outer size, inner size and depth. Whole millimetres from the main scale, fractions from the **vernier**: find the line that lines up.
- **Thread:** measure the major diameter, use a **pitch gauge** for the pitch, and look up the standard in a table.
- **Detailing** means pulling one part out of an assembly and drawing it so it can be made from the drawing alone.
- A part drawing needs: **the necessary views and sections, all dimensions, tolerances, surface roughness, manufacturing notes**.

## Vernier caliper

Analogy: the main scale is the hour hand, the vernier is the minute hand. The main scale says "55 and a bit", the vernier says exactly how much "a bit" is.

1. See where the **vernier's zero** sits on the main scale. The whole millimetres to its left, e.g. **55**.
2. Find the vernier line that **lines up exactly** with a main-scale line.
3. That line's number is the fraction. The vernier step is usually **0.05 mm**, so the 5th line means 0.25.
4. Total **55.25**.

The lower jaws measure outside sizes, the upper "ears" inside sizes, the tail at the back depth. The second scale is often inches.

## Identifying a thread

1. Measure the **major diameter** with the caliper (for a nut, the inside).
2. **Thread pitch gauge**: a set of toothed blades. Try them one by one until one sits in the thread **with no light showing**. Its pitch is stamped on it.
3. Look up the standard: diameter 10 and pitch 1.5 is **M10**. Diameter 12.7 with 13 threads per inch is 1/2" UNC.
4. Trapezoidal and other lead-screw threads are identified the same way from their own tables.

## Detailing from an assembly (homework 3)

Task: from an assembly drawing (torque limiter coupling, fuel valve, mechanical jack, etc.) produce **drawings of three parts**.

Steps:

1. **Part zero: understand how the assembly works.** What turns, what moves, what holds what.
2. **Colour the parts** on a copy of the assembly. Seminar 8 showed why: opposite hatching helps, but with colour you instantly see where one part ends and the next begins.
3. Choose the **front view** for each part separately (not necessarily as in the assembly; a shaft always lies horizontal).
4. Decide which **views and sections** are needed. An extra view is as wrong as a missing one.
5. Give **all dimensions**, measured off the assembly drawing taking the scale into account.
6. Add **tolerances** where parts mate (fits from the assembly go onto the parts: the hole gets H7, the shaft g6, and so on), **roughness** and **manufacturing notes**.
7. Freehand first, discuss with the teacher, then with instruments on A4 or A3.

## Surface roughness (briefly)

A part drawing says how smooth each surface must be. The seminar split it roughly into three levels:

| Level | Where |
|---|---|
| **Rough / coarse** | Non-mating surfaces, as-cast or rough-machined |
| **Smooth** | Ordinary machined surfaces, end faces, chamfers |
| **Fine** | Mating surfaces: bearing seats, bushing seats, seals |

The part's general roughness goes in the top-right corner (or by the title block); special values go right on the surface.

## Homework 4: pneumatic cylinder

The biggest homework (up to 25 points). You get a real disassembled pneumatic cylinder unit.

1. **Part zero:** understand the shapes and how the parts connect.
2. **Part one:** freehand sketches, **dimensions measured off the parts** with caliper and pitch gauge. No layout.
3. **Part two:** freehand part drawings on A4/A3, with dimensions, tolerances and roughness.
4. **Part three:** freehand assembly drawing of the piston.
5. **Part four:** constructed assembly drawing. Everything is handed in **together**, and the teacher approves each step.
