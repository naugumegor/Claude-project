## The whole topic in a minute

- Insides in a normal view are a mess of dashed lines. So you mentally **cut** the part and remove the front half.
- A **section** shows the cut face and everything visible behind it. A **cut (profile)** shows only the cut face.
- Cut material is **hatched** with narrow lines at 45°. Neighbouring parts get opposite directions or different spacing.
- **Never cut lengthwise:** shafts, pins, bolts, keys, balls and rollers, ribs, spokes, teeth. They stay as a view.
- Hollow turned parts are drawn **half view – half section**: the section goes below or to the right.

## Why cut

![Flanged bushing: view, full section, half view – half section](/drawing/sections.svg "One bushing drawn three ways")

Analogy: to show the filling of a pie you don't draw dashed lines through the crust, you **slice** it and show the inside. Same on a drawing. Dashed lines only help with the shape; the inner build-up is shown by a section.

How a section is made: a **cutting plane** slices the part, the piece between the plane and your eye is removed, and the rest is projected as usual.

## Hatching

- Narrow continuous parallel lines, equally spaced, normally **at 45°**.
- If the hatching would run parallel to an outline, use **30° or 60°**.
- A bigger part gets **wider** spacing.
- One part is hatched **the same way** in every view and section, even in an enlarged detail.
- Neighbouring parts in an assembly: **different direction** or spacing, or you can't tell where one ends.
- A section thinner than 2 mm may be **filled in solid**.
- If a dimension figure lands on hatching, break the hatching around it (on hand drawings).
- Large parts may be hatched only in a band **along the outline**.
- Non-metals can have special hatch patterns, but 45° lines mean metal or "material in general".
- A welded seam in section is **filled black**; a glued joint is a wide line.

## What must not be cut lengthwise

Rule: cut only if the section gives **new information**. Cut a solid shaft lengthwise and you get a hatched rectangle, nothing new.

**Never cut along the axis:** solid shafts, axles, pins, bolts, screws, rivets, keys, dowels, bearing balls and rollers, gear teeth.

**Thin features that don't run all the way round:** ribs, wheel spokes, teeth. If the plane runs along a rib, draw the rib **unhatched**, as if it weren't cut. Otherwise the rib looks like a solid thick disc, which is a lie.

**Standard fasteners** (nuts, washers, bolts) don't need to be sectioned; they usually stay as a view.

Cutting all of these **across** is fine: a cross-cut of a shaft shows its keyseat, which is useful.

## Types of sections

| Section | When | How to label |
|---|---|---|
| **Full section** | One plane through the whole part | On the symmetry plane the label can be left out |
| **Half view – half section** | Symmetric hollow turned parts | Section **below** the axis or **to the right**, split along the centre line |
| **Broken-out (local)** | Show a small bit of the inside | Boundary is a freehand narrow line, no label needed |
| **Offset** | Several parallel planes | Hatching continuous, as if it were one plane |
| **Revolved (aligned)** | Planes at an angle, e.g. through holes on a flange | The angled part is turned into the common plane |

**Labelling:** if the plane isn't obvious, show it as a narrow long-dash dotted line with **short wide strokes at the ends** and arrows for the viewing direction. Letters next to it, and **A–A** above the section. If the section's scale differs, add it: **A–A (2:1)**. Identical sections may share a letter.

## Cuts (profiles)

A cut shows **only** what lies in the plane itself, nothing behind it.

- **Revolved cut:** drawn right on the view, turned 90°, with **narrow** lines. Handy for spokes, levers, rolled sections.
- **Removed cut:** drawn next to the view, on the extension of the cutting line, with **wide** lines. This is how a keyseat on a shaft is shown.
- A cut must not fall apart into **separate pieces**. If it does, you need a section instead.
